To your above statements:

- cloning the package under the GPL does not make anyone agree not to 
share. Your analogy does not fit.

- forking is sharing. The Manifesto says everyone will be permitted to 
modify and redistribute, and that the fundamental act of friendship 
among programmers is the sharing of programs. A fork that keeps the 
license intact is the Golden Rule in action, not a violation of it.

- Contribution is a freedom, not a duty. Making and distributing copies 
is explicitly not an obligation. "Instead of contributing to them" 
assumes a duty that free software does not impose.

And I do not see how Neomacs divides community, as they do not speak of 
any radical politics to reject X, or otherwise will be consequence Y -- 
they made a fork, and shared it with the world. Great thing. If the 
feature of "fully threaded Emacs" would really work well, and Emacs 
maintainers can study it, such a feature could be implemented in Emacs. 
By sharing the software, they have already contributed back.

The only problem is in copyrights, and in that case the software can be 
re-written to be used.

When "Human" Emacs forks -- that means copyrights will not be assigned 
to the FSF, so FSF cannot easily accept such "Human" Emacs patches 
unless they are assigned to the FSF.

"Human" Emacs and the GNU Emacs, as conscious contributors wish to share 
software for the benefit of majority of users, not just those using 
"Human" Emacs.

Open Source camp is defined by refusing to treat the issue as justice to 
users and arguing from practical benefits only.

And the Golden Rule is about the sharing not about learning from 
existing code.

Programmers have always learned by copying: reading other people's code, 
borrowing patterns, reusing snippets. Stallman says this copying is as 
natural and as productive as breathing, and it should be free.

An LLM does the same thing, only at enormous scale. It was built by 
absorbing code that people shared, and it reproduces patterns from that 
shared body of work. So the model is not against the Manifesto's view of 
copying — it is that same act, magnified.

The model is a machine that copies at industrial scale, and copying is 
exactly what the Manifesto defends as natural, productive, and free.

Conditionally, that all the software, datasets, drivers and firmware are 
free too.

> GNU was built by and for hackers, and the hacker spirit is to

"LLM ideology" belongs to the companies and users, not the model. The 
Manifesto addresses agents who can choose — someone who can refuse an 
NDA, share a program, or be punished for restricting use. A model cannot

Individualism is not the opposite of sharing. The opposite of sharing is 
restriction, not independence. Writing code alone and releasing it under 
the GPL is sharing.

And Open Source camp doesn't restrict sharing. Its actual flaw is amoral 
framing: it declines to see the issue as justice to users and argues 
from practical benefits only.

Technical improvement is not the enemy. Problem is when people choose 
technical improvement on technical grounds alone, when that choice means 
accepting a nonfree program.

I would like to express my confusion why this is being discussed like a 
politics issue rather than a code of conduct issue,

The argument I quoted baffled me because since with the same line of 
reasoning, I feel like one could decline any sort of Code of Conduct as 
too radical and that it would split communities.


Now I understand that on some level this would make sense, if it really 
wasn't relevant to the project itself, but that feels arguably untrue:

1. The copyright questions seem to continue to be unresolved, even for 
some small snippets: 
https://fsfe.org/news/2026/news-20260825-01.en.html (I'm not a lawyer so 
I can't judge this for myself, but I'd like to additionally express my 
confusion why this article doesn't conclude "it seems best for projects 
to not touch this tech for now"..)

2. It seems like AI can real harm on its users. Now you might argue you 
can avoid it personally, but can everyone? You could say its the 
responsibility of every contributor, but that may not always work out, 
so I think a risk for the project itself is undeniable, even if low.

3. Higher risk for long term maintenance also seem pretty clear, since 
human reviews apparently often can't keep up with the amount of code LLM 
contributors produce.

4. I saw elsewhere the comparison you don't ask if contributors support 
slave labor in other ventures. But surely that's a bit different from 
allowing some form of slave labor product to directly knowingly 
contribute to your project. (Not just people who have that view, but a 
product, often proprietary, that has this known association, directly 
altering the code base.) I feel like that's a more direct impact worth 
considering on a local project level.


Now I'm not saying the logical conclusion is an LLM ban.[1]

I just don't get these arguments that it is naturally out of scope.

And downstream users can still do with the code whatever they want, 
including using LLMs with it, so this doesn't feel very restrictive.

I think an additional thing to consider here is that any choice, 
including no AI policy or a lenient policy, may also split the 
community, maybe even worse so.

My apologies if I'm simply misunderstanding something here.

[1]: I won't hide that I personally think that is the logical 
conclusion. But that's really besides the point of my inquiry.

Yes.  To elaborate more about excluding base .el file names rather than
just the vc package name, I think it is for cases like this:

Repository A:

./A
├── A-1.el      ;; Package-Requires: ((A "0"))
├── A-2.el      ;; Package-Requires: ((A-1 "0") (B "0"))
└── A.el        ;; Package-Requires: ()

Where "A", "A-1", "A-2" are 3 packages on an archive.

Without excluding, installing vc "A" will end up installing tarball
packages "A", "A-1" in addition to vc "A" (B is always installed).  By
checking file names, we prevent installing tarball "A-1", better than
just excluding "A" by package names.
