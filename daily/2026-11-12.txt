﻿I've followed at a distance some of the research on this over the last 
few years. From what I've seen, the research consistently concludes that 
LLMs hurt learning and overall cognition. Here's a selection of papers 
on the topic.

First is "How AI Impacts Skill Formation" 
<https://arxiv.org/pdf/2601.20245>. This was published by Anthropic 
researchers in Feb 2026 and compares users learning a new Python library 
by themselves or with a chatbot they can query, so it's closely relevant 
to our situation. (Since it's Anthropic, if there's any bias, it's 
likely in favor of LLMs as a useful assistant for programming.)

Their top-line results show a statistically-insignificant reduction in 
task completion time (23min with AI, 25 without, p=0.391), but the 
reduction in test score *is* statistically-significant (50% with AI, 66% 
without, p=0.010).

Before I go any farther, I should note that this is a fairly small 
study, at n=52 (26 each in the AI and no-AI groups). However, there are 
a few interesting details beyond the top-line results. First, there's 
almost no difference in task completion time, except for novices (1-3 
years experience). See Figure 7. Broken down by age, the average test 
score is the reverse: very close for 1-3 years experience, but wider for 
other levels (again though, this is statistically weak due to the small 
population size).

The study also suggests there are a few interaction patterns that can 
produce better results: "Generation-Then-Comprehension", "Hybrid 
Code-Explanation", and "Conceptual Inquiry" (see Figure 11 and the 
nearby text). Compared to the lower-scoring patterns, these ones had 
greater emphasis on asking questions than having the chatbot do the 
work. *However*, these groups are n=2, n=3, and n=7 respectively, and 
I'll additionally note that Fig 11 has no error bars. Statistically 
speaking, I don't think you can put much weight on this; again, even the 
full population is quite small at n=52.

Finally, the authors also note in Section 7.1 that, "This should be a 
lower bound for cognitive offloading since agentic AI coding tools would 
require even less human participation." A common objection with research 
like this is that the tools are so much more advanced now, but the 
authors hypothesize here that these advancements would make it even 
harder to learn. (And in any case, marketing doesn't override science 
just because it has a more recent publication date.)

Here are some additional papers with similar results. I haven't looked 
at these in as much detail, so I'm only providing a quick blurb about each:

Endoscopist deskilling risk after exposure to artificial intelligence in 
colonoscopy: a multicentre, observational study (Oct 2025)
<https://doi.org/10.1016/S2468-1253(25)00133-5>

This paper observed the endoscopists had worse performance doing 
non-AI-assisted colonoscopies after having spent 3 months using AI 
assistance.

Thinking—Fast, Slow, and Artificial: How AI is Reshaping Human Reasoning 
and the Rise of Cognitive Surrender (Feb 2026)
<https://doi.org/10.31234/osf.io/yk25n_v1>

This paper introduces the idea of "cognitive surrender": when using AI, 
users "often surrender to its outputs—whether correct or flawed."

----------------------------------------

Sycophantic AI decreases prosocial intentions and promotes dependence 
(Mar 2026)
<https://doi.org/10.1126/science.aec8352>

This one is about interpersonal interactions, but the results are quite 
disturbing: "In our human experiments, even a single interaction with 
sycophantic AI reduced participants’ willingness to take responsibility 
and repair interpersonal conflicts, while increasing their own 
conviction that they were right. Yet despite distorting judgment, 
sycophantic models were trusted and preferred."

----------------------------------------

AI Tools in Society: Impacts on Cognitive Offloading and the Future of 
Critical Thinking (Jan 2025)
<https://doi.org/10.3390/soc15010006>

I believe this paper is mostly based on self-reports from the study 
participants, so you may want to take it with a grain of salt. Still, 
the authors conclude: "The findings revealed a significant negative 
correlation between frequent AI tool usage and critical thinking 
abilities, mediated by increased cognitive offloading."

----------------------------------------

AI-overdependence and human cognitive decline: Hazards, evidence, and 
mitigation strategies (May 2026)
<https://doi.org/10.1016/j.chbr.2026.101102>

This paper reviews the literature on AI's cognitive effects over the 
last few years, and according to the authors, "reveals that 
AI-overdependence poses a significant threat to higher-order cognitive 
skills, especially critical thinking, complex decision-making, and 
analytical reasoning, while also undermining creativity, memory, and 
executive functions. These effects stem from well-documented mechanisms 
such as cognitive offloading, metacognitive laziness, and automation 
bias, compounded by the perceived authority and convenience of AI systems."

----------------------------------------

 From Offloading to Engagement: An Experimental Study on Structured 
Prompting and Critical Reasoning with Generative AI (Oct 2025)
<https://doi.org/10.3390/data10110172>

This is the most-positive paper I could find after a brief search, and 
suggests that "structured prompting" avoids some of the problems with 
cognitive offloading. This structured prompting consists of restricting 
LLM use to looking up contextual or factual information, and then later 
prompting the LLM to generate counterarguments for their essay. However, 
the study only uses self-reporting to measure the level of cognitive 
offloading, so I'd again take this with a grain of salt. Even with the 
comparatively optimistic views of the author, they note, "The study 
highlights this risk of an illusion of non-offloading: even individuals 
with strong critical dispositions may fail to recognise their reliance 
on AI, accepting outputs as anchors while believing they have remained 
independent."

----------------------------------------

While it takes a long time to come to true scientific consensus, 
especially with a technology like LLMs that are seeing such frequent 
changes, current research is pointing towards LLM use having significant 
cognitive and learning harms. Given all of that, I prefer to apply the 
precautionary principle. Maybe in the future, the situation will change, 
but I'd rather wait until there's a reasonable amount of research that 
has happier results than the above papers.

I rejected the explanations of the root cause(s) of the problem
because they made no sense to me, given my familiarity with how the
display engine works.  I also pointed out why the explanations made no
sense.  Since they made no sense, whether to accept patches was never
on the table, because the patches were based on assumptions and
conclusions which couldn't be correct, as they disregarded basic
principles of how the display engine works.

In general, both in my work on Emacs and elsewhere in my life, I never
completely reject any idea or utterance, because almost always there's
something useful in there.  Even if the idea is completely false, it
can frequently give me correct ideas as I think about why and how it
is false.  The source of the ideas is never too important (except that
if I know from experience that a particular source has high
probability of generating false ideas, I will scrutinize ideas from
such a source with some prejudice).  Therefore, I cannot agree with
people who say that LLM-generated text should be ignored as garbage,
because it could include useful parts or lead me to useful ideas as I
consider what it tells.

That's what I did in this case: rejected the wrong ideas and the
patches offered based on those wrong ideas after showing with code
fragments why they cannot be true.  Thus, "talking to LLM" allowed me
to examine the code in Emacs and attempt to find problems there which
could explain the issues discussed in that bug report.

Note that, among a lot of incorrect hypotheses and suggestions which
were posted "from LLM's mouth" in that discussion, one was close to
the real cause of the problem, but failed to identify the place where
the code should be corrected.  That place was later identified by
Sameer Singh and the problem was fixed where it happens.  My
conclusion from this was that thinking about what LLM tells is useful,
because some, even if small, part of what it says could give useful
ideas.  When dealing with code in Emacs we are less familiar with,
that could help identify and solve problems which otherwise might
remain unsolved.

  >  It is the amounts of code team will have to process and 
  > maintain - LLMs make it that the act of "writing the code" is the easy 
  > part, which allows a lot more code to be submitted

We do the best we can feasibly do, with the pile of bug reports and
patches we receive.

Just because a person sends us a proposed change, it does not follow
that we _have to_ look at it.  When we do look at a change, it does
not follow that we _have to_ study it until we understand it.  We
could instead let it drop.

If a message shows us there is a bug, and presents a proposed fix, we
can use that fix if we can understand it and be sure it is correct.
We also have the possibility of fixing that bug another way, if that
is easier.  If we can't understand it, we may have to let it drop.

  > IMO, it's a point to be discussed, hence why I brought it up - if the 
  > team is collectively OK with that they will have to deal with code 
  > submission rates growing up at the rates LLMs allow for, then this point 
  > can be dismissed.

I think these are independent issues:

1. Will people send us more proposed patches because THEY use LLMs?

2. Will Emacs get a lot bigger because of OUR installing patches
made by LLMs?
