On 11/6/26 12:44 PM, Eli Zaretskii wrote:
> And what costs are you alluding to that need to be considered should
> we be allowed to use LLMs in Emacs?  If someone uses LLM to generate
> some part of a patch, what costs will that impose on anyone else?
There are several costs, but I think the most important one here is the 
asymmetry cost.  The author who uses an LLM uses it to reduce the effort 
of understanding the problem, and shifts that effort onto the reviewers 
and accepters of the patch.  Even if the patch is ultimately rejected, 
the reviewer still has to spend the effort to determine that it cannot 
be understood or reorganized, and the author cannot help because they 
don't understand it either.  Moreover, for that effort to be worthwhile 
on the maintainer's side, the maintainer must also be able to produce 
and review code with an LLM, which means they must control that LLM in 
order to be effective as a maintainer.

Richard Stallman <rms@gnu.org> writes:

> This is a rather disturbing picture.  It suggests that maintaining a
> program whose code was generated by LLM is a complex quandry: what
> constitutes "the source code"?  Is it the output generated by the LLM,
> or is it the input to the LLM system?  Is either of the two really
> usable as the source code?
>
> If the output, generated in some programming language, is
> understandable as source code, and reasonable to modify,
> that is ok.  We could designate that as "the source code".
>
> However, it may be difficult to edit by hand.

I have worked at two different software companies where they have
welcomed in LLM contributions, so I have firsthand experience observing
what happens to a codebase where this is allowed. In both cases, the
developers were very clearly instructed by management to use it
"responsibly" and to keep the source code maintainable.

The developers started out with the best intentions and initially used
it to make edits that were reasonable in scope. But in both cases it
didn't end there; the more they used these systems, the more comfortable
they got with putting them in the driver's seat, and over time the
systems decayed to the point where it was an exhausting feat to try to
understand them.

I would ask my co-workers what a function that they ostensibly wrote was
meant to do, and I'd get blank stares back. Eventually I had to leave
the first company (during the worst job market of my entire life) due to
this problem.

This phenomenon is known as the "vibe bobsled", and it's been well
documented; even people who are enthusiastic LLM users admit it happens:

https://dustycloud.org/blog/faulty-towers-vibe-sickness-and-the-vibe-bobsled/

> At the top of the chute, people tell themselves they're going to use
> these tools as a kind of fancy autocomplete. As they descend, they say
> they'll spin up some agents to explore ideas, but they'll write the
> code themselves. Next their agents are generating the code for them,
> but don't worry, but they'll review all the output. Soon they're
> plummeting downward and well, they don't actually review the code
> being spat out much anymore, but they trust the agents, heck maybe the
> agents are actually better coders than they are they say. And where
> does it go from there? From "I don't even code anymore" to "I don't
> even prompt anymore"?
>
> At every stage of the process, the coder in question removes
> themselves from the process of producing code, and gives in towards a
> faith-based initiative of code production, that the LLM knows and does
> a good job of what it's doing. But what is the source of gravity
> pulling the sled along this icy chute?
>
> It's simple. Generation is not the slow part of
> coding. Theory-building and review are. And plausible-enough things
> are extremely hard to debug and understand. But the machines are so
> fast at producing things. If you are going to review their work, you
> aren't really taking advantage of their most powerful property, which
> is speed. But theory-building and review are also the programmer's
> most important role.

Like with many SaaS products, LLM vendors have been caught admitting
that they deliberately design the flow of these systems with the goal of
being addictive, so it's not particularly surprising that this happens.
The only surprise is that people who should know better continue to fall
for it.

I know this looks at first glance like a slippery slope fallacy, but I'm
just describing what happened from painful firsthand personal experience.
I have read about the same thing happening in multiple free software
projects as well, but I can only speak from firsthand experience what
it's like at work, because the free software projects I contribute to
other than Emacs have already taken steps to ban this.

-Phil

ps. I hesitate to bring this up at all, because I believe the ethical
    issues involved are much, much more serious than the practical
    issues. I believe GNU should have a hardline stance against
    plagiarism, and it's baffling to me that this is controversial! But
    I made an exception here because RMS asked about it specifically.
    Please do not interpret this as implying that it would be OK to
    accept LLM patches if the issues above were somehow comprehensively
    addressed!

The blank stares happen in the workplace because we frequently spend
time in video calls. On a mailing list it's not real-time, so of course
the LLM user would have a chance to copy your questions into their LLM
and feed the slop replies back to you.

It's quite difficult to explain if you haven't experienced it firsthand,
but LLM-generated code and explanations are astonishingly good at
*looking* correct and being extremely convincing at first glance in a
way that is completely disconnected to whether it is correct or not.

Imagine dealing with a never-ending stream of subtly-flawed patches
written by expert liars trying to trick you into accepting them. Your
intuition that you've spent decades building up based on reading code
written by humans is nearly worthless in this case, because it's been
fed trillions of examples of training data of what a convincing reply
looks like and can emit a realistic-looking reproduction of them.

That's the entire strength of an LLM; it can generate convincing-looking
text at an inexhaustible pace. On occasion by luck sometimes it happens
that it aligns with correctness, but it will always *appear* correct.

-Phil

I don't want to fork GNU Emacs either, and absolutely agree that doing

people feel very strongly about, and which affects both how (free) 
software is produced and how community members work together. I fear

For my part, I spoke up in this thread rather than waiting because I 
want to raise these concerns before any serious problems befall the

other projects' struggles than to be thrown into managing a possible

I don't think our views are so far apart here. It's why in my message, I 
was careful to focus on LLM-assisted contributions instead of LLMs in 
general. In addition to the legal/copyright issues that the FSF is 
already aware of, LLMs significantly alter the way communication 
happens, whether that's code, documentation, or discussions on the 
mailing lists.

Take the recent bug reports from "roelof's assistent", which 
self-reports as an AI. So long as this is a rare occurrence, it's no 
reason to panic, but I imagine it would get frustrating pretty quickly 
if this were a common way to report bugs. At best, it's an extra layer 
between us and the user making it harder to build a human connection; at 
worst, it's spam contributing to maintainer burnout.

Ultimately, while I have my own opnions about LLMs in general, I agree 
that it would be quite a serious change to start rejecting someone's 
contributions for using LLMs in some other context, or even simply 
having different views on them.

> If some contributor decides to use LLMs for developing Emacs code, and
> they do it in a way that prevents them from learning, that is their
> business: the harm, such as it is, will be caused to them and no one
> else.  Our job is to make sure that code that lands in Emacs is
> useful, correct, well-designed and well-implemented.  We cannot be
> held responsible for whatever the contributors do in the privacy of
> their development environment.  I also think it's naïve, to say the
> least, to think that by rejecting LLM-generated code we will affect
> their decision to use LLM.

We wouldn't likely change anyone's mind (certainly not quickly), but we 
*can* do things to encourage participation from the sorts of community 
members we'd like to have.

> We don't require knowledgeable Emacs developers to provide mentoring.
> I personally think we should try (and do try myself), but this is not
> a requirement.

That's true, and I've wanted to provide mentorship for newcomers in a 
more active way, though I'm still thinking about how best to do that. I 
credit the friendliness and mentorship of you, Lars Ingebrigtsen, 
Michael Albinus, and others for why I maintain Eshell now, despite not 
knowing any Lisp as recently as 2021. That's one of the main reasons 
this aspect is so important to me. The mentorship and community in Emacs 
is something everyone here should be proud of.

> So I don't see why the (so far largely theoretical) danger that
> someone will mentor less contributors who use LLMs is so fundamentally
> different from them deciding to mentor less because they have less
> free time or for whatever other reason.

There are a few factors that worry me about the intersection of LLMs and 
mentorship. First, as I mentioned before, is the marketing. If we listen 
to the companies making these products, the intent is for them to 
replace junior developers, and that will be reflected in their design.

Second is their addictive nature: because LLMs are stochastic and don't 
always produce correct results, users experience a variable reward 
schedule, leading to addiction in the same ways gambling can. This often 
leads to overuse at the expense of less immediately-satisfying things, 
like mentoring. (These problems aren't strictly unique to LLMs, but I 
think the risks are elevated with them.)

Third, like you mentioned, the attitude of the mentee counts. A mentee 
who not only wants to learn but also to engage socially is more 
satisfying to mentor. One of the complaints the Linux kernel maintainers 
had was that LLM-using novices are particularly frustrating to mentor, 
which was one of their reasons for deprioritizing mentorship. This is 
partly because they don't learn and partly because they just want their 
name in the kernel changelog.

I don't expect the Emacs maintainers or the community as a whole to make 
a decision on this right away. I also hope you don't see Human Emacs as 
a power play or personality conflict. For me at least, I view it as a 
last resort to preserve a space for people who value the craft of code 
and the community built around it.

This argment holds if afterwards no one will use LLMs to work on the
code.

An example where this would be the case could be when someone takes up
an abandoned project.
(though this doesn’t resolve the copyright problems)

If non-LLM-use isn’t secured, there is a risk that taking up such a
project creates pressure to use LLMs to work with the code.

Or that it could open the loophole of saying that this project was only
generated as a start and now will be fit for humans – but then more
LLM-generated changes are added, again creating pressure to use LLMs.

> The code needs comments and clarity sufficient for
> humans to work on it.

This could be stated more in a more general way:
the code should be in suitable shape for humans to work with it.

It should at least be maintainable without pushing people into depending
on SaaSS or proprietary tools to provide the minimum of maintenance
required when pressing matters like security issues come up.

One part is that it should be small enough: with LLMs one human can
within months cause the generation of 100.000 lines of code that do
something. It is unlikely that this amount of LLM-created code could be
understood by a group of humans, and even less likely that it could be
maintained.

This is different with human-written projects, because even if those
grow big, their different parts were at least partially understood by a
human by the time they were written.

> Using LLM input as source code will require that the specific LLM be
> free, released, and usable locally, and acceptable to require
> developers to use.

Another part there is that the input should actually work as source
code. Meaning that the LLM can be run again and no repeat review of the
code is needed. Input in that case would not just be prompts, but the
whole state of the LLM. A minimum question to that would be “can you
reproduce the results from the source code”. In the past years we (as
Free Software community) spent a lot of effort to make most of our
binary builds reproducible. Similar may be needed to use LLMs as a kind
of transpiler.

If the LLM is Free Software, it would also be necessary that its source
is at least provided on request. And the source of current LLMs is
massive: roughly all of the internet, along with filters and "alignment"
measures, plus build tooling to process it in data centers for weeks to
months, likely burning through some expensive GPUs.

On that note, the observation discussed in the blogpost of a growing
amount of code that nobody understands leading to eventually
surrendering to the LLM to explain things came from an LLM *proponent*,
which I used as the frame. Whereas I described a bobsled, the original
proponent described a growing tower... Armin Ronacher, one of the
biggest generative AI via LLM in coding proponents currently around
wrote a blogpost called "The Tower Keeps Rising":

  https://lucumr.pocoo.org/2026/7/13/the-tower-keeps-rising/

In it, Armin describes that AI-assisted codebases eventually become
incomprehensible to human participants, and that they keep growing,
and this is not unlike the tower of babel, which as it grows the
people involved cannot communicate with each other. However, Armin said
this is no problem, for the LLM which is generating the code itself
provides the solution: instead of you, the human, deigning to understand
the codebase, you eventually come to rely on the AI to explain something
when you need it explained. And so it is no problem that we stop
communicating with each other, that humans become less involved with the
process: the tower keeps rising, and when you need to understand
something, you ask the LLM-AI based agent instead.

This was from a *proponent*, mind you.

So the main difference between my blogpost describing the "vibe bobsled"
and Armin's is that Armin is a proponent, and described this as upward
growth. But myself, being worried about communities and the individual
of people to communicate, describe it as downward movement.

In both cases, Armin and I do agree on something: that codebases which
use these things become incomprehensible to people, who come to rely on
AI agents to instead take over. It's merely whether you consider that
upward progress or downward decline that we disagree on.
