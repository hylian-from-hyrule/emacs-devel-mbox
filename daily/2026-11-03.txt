I'm also a signatory on Human Emacs. For my part, I don't want to block 
all discussion, though I'd rather avoid rehashing introductory 
explanations about common ethical concerns. People can easily find these 
elsewhere online to learn about them, even if they don't end up agreeing.

I hope that forking won't be necessary, because I think the most 
valuable thing Emacs (and GNU and the FSF as a whole) has is its 
community. Splitting that community would be a tragedy. However, I also 
strongly believe that a permissive stance towards LLM-assisted 
contributions will damage this very same community even more profoundly 
in the long run. I hope to do what I can to preserve it.

Research has repeatedly shown that using LLMs to generate the results 
that someone wants will undermine their ability to learn new things, 
depriving themselves and the larger community from this hard-won 
knowledge. This is even more problematic for novices, who may have never 
had an opportunity to learn things the "old-fashioned" way. Worse still, 
the LLM providers advertise their models as equivalent to a junior 
engineer, encouraging maintainers to forego the work of mentoring novice 
contributors; even the Linux kernel has now decided to abandon 
mentorship, arguing that you must already be an expert or be prepared to 
spend lots of money on LLM tokens.

Working with LLMs also encourages isolation. According to the LLM 
providers, we should each be "orchestrating" a team of "agents" to do 
all our work; if there's a more socially-atomized way of producing 
software, I can't imagine what it is. Even LLM proponents I know have 
raised this concern, but have been unable to offer a solution. This 
echoes Mark Zuckerberg's assertion that people would like more friends, 
so his company will happily provide that in the form of chatbot "partners".

I've seen these problems play out in projects I'm involved with, 
projects I rely on, and projects I only hear about when the problems 
boil over. I've thus concluded that LLMs corrode the community practices 
that free software relies on. I'd hate to see Emacs, one of the projects 
that best embodies the vision of a different way of computing, to suffer 
the same fate.

> Research has repeatedly shown that using LLMs to generate the results

If some contributor decides to use LLMs for developing Emacs code, and
they do it in a way that prevents them from learning, that is their
business: the harm, such as it is, will be caused to them and no one
else.  Our job is to make sure that code that lands in Emacs is
useful, correct, well-designed and well-implemented.  We cannot be
held responsible for whatever the contributors do in the privacy of
their development environment.  I also think it's naïve, to say the
least, to think that by rejecting LLM-generated code we will affect
their decision to use LLM.

> Worse still, the LLM providers advertise their models as equivalent
> to a junior engineer, encouraging maintainers to forego the work of
> mentoring novice contributors; even the Linux kernel has now decided
> to abandon mentorship, arguing that you must already be an expert or
> be prepared to spend lots of money on LLM tokens.

We don't require knowledgeable Emacs developers to provide mentoring.
I personally think we should try (and do try myself), but this is not
a requirement.  It is subject to the decisions and free time resources
of each one of us.  We also have Real Lives, and frequently we see
someone who was very active in our forums to become much less active
or even disappear for prolonged periods of time; that also affects
mentoring.  Last, but not least, not every contributor reacts nicely
to mentoring; 'nough said.

All this happens _today_.  The result is that the level of mentoring
we provide is inconsistent and extremely variable.  And we live with
that, because frankly, what else could we do?

So I don't see why the (so far largely theoretical) danger that
someone will mentor less contributors who use LLMs is so fundamentally
different from them deciding to mentor less because they have less
free time or for whatever other reason.

And again, at least for me, the desire to mentor depends heavily on
the attitude of the mentoree -- if I see them eager to learn, I will
try harder, and if I see they don't like to be taught or don't want to
learn, I will stop.  It doesn't matter for me why they stop -- it's
their funeral, not mine and not Emacs's.  People should be free to do
what they feel is right for them, even if I think they are mistaken.

> Working with LLMs also encourages isolation. According to the LLM 
> providers, we should each be "orchestrating" a team of "agents" to do 
> all our work; if there's a more socially-atomized way of producing 
> software, I can't imagine what it is. Even LLM proponents I know have 
> raised this concern, but have been unable to offer a solution. This 
> echoes Mark Zuckerberg's assertion that people would like more friends, 
> so his company will happily provide that in the form of chatbot "partners".

Again, that's not our concern.  And again, it would be naïve to assume
that rejecting LLM-generated code will cause people become more
socialized.  Because if they want to use LLMs for Emacs, they probably
do that in umpteen other places, and if that makes them more
"isolated", nothing we can do or say is likely to change that.

> I've seen these problems play out in projects I'm involved with, 
> projects I rely on, and projects I only hear about when the problems 
> boil over. I've thus concluded that LLMs corrode the community practices 
> that free software relies on. I'd hate to see Emacs, one of the projects 
> that best embodies the vision of a different way of computing, to suffer 
> the same fate.

Please at least wait until there's some sign of something like that
happening in Emacs.  It is at least unfair to threaten a fork (which
110% sure is a blow to the community -- I'm old enough to remember how
things were during the XEmacs schism) just because the Linux kernel
did something we don't like or don't want to happen in our community.

Bottom line, I think these concerns are very premature, and in most
aspects simply not relevant to Emacs development.  To me, the actual
danger is the threat to fork as a kind of "precaution" against
problems that either didn't yet happen and are extremely unlikely to
happen, or are happening already due to a variety of real-life
reasons without causing any fuss.

And I'm most alarmed by the suggestion that we should reject someone's
code based on something that is unrelated to the code itself.  This is
a step along a slippery slope that I very much hope we will never
take.  Because the next thing we will be requested is to reject code
of people with particular views or opinions, or people living under
certain regimes, etc. etc.  We never did anything like that, and
IMNSHO we shouldn't, ever.  The world out there is full of grave
problems that need to be solved, but Emacs development is not the
right place to solve almost all of these problems.
