concerns about the future of the project. As we see other projects
adopting policies which accept LLM contributions, it's encouraged us to
see that the current Emacs policy disallows them.

Our concerns stem from the temporary nature of the current policy, and its
apparent focus on resolving legal uncertainty surrounding the copyright
status of LLM-generated source code. Ensuring that the copyright of Emacs is
legally defensible is of course important, but the way it appears to form the
crux of the decision overlooks the harm posed to users and communities by
acceptance of LLM-generated code. It would be a mistake to say that because
something is legally allowable it must be acceptable, and for many of us who
are deeply invested in Emacs, it's not a mistake we'd expect from this
project's leadership.

Free Software is more than just code. It's built on a foundation of trust and
solidarity. The greatest strength of Emacs is not its technology but the user
base which shares and works together. As we watch this discussion play out in
other communities which have not taken a strong stance, we've seen the
corrosive effect that LLMs have on trust and solidarity as soon as they are
allowed in. We still have the chance to avoid that in the Emacs community and
learn from the mistakes of others!

(At this point I should clarify that an argument about whether these harms
are serious is not my interest; so if you are opening your MUA to compose a
reply on these lines, please consider that we've heard all the arguments
already and remain unmoved by them. We're not interested in arguments about
how powerful they are, nor about how local models will some day address the
problems, nor about how people can lie about the provenance of their
patches; none of these are relevant. You're welcome to discuss this on a
separate thread.)

In a previous thread[1] that raised the question of the Emacs policy, one of
the Emacs maintainers wrote:

> Such policy discussions and decisions are not for the Emacs project to
> make, they are global for all the GNU packages, and so here is the wrong
> place to look for them.

While it's true that this mailing list cannot make policy decisions for all
of GNU, there is no reason that Emacs could not hold itself to a higher
standard than the rest of GNU. If the GNU-wide ban is relaxed, that is not
the same thing as GNU demanding that all projects accept LLM-generated
contributions; it only means that they *may* if they so decide.

Our goal in reaching out is twofold:

First, we want the maintainers of Emacs to be aware of our intention to use
a version of Emacs which is fully free from LLM contributions regardless of
whether the current ban is made permanent or lifted. We invite those making
the decision to look beyond copyright and weigh ALL the harms that lifting
such a ban would inflict. Our fond hope is that upon consideration of the
issues, Emacs maintainers will replace the temporary policy with a
commitment to comprehensive prohibition of all LLM contributions including
but not limited to code, documentation, commit messages.

We consider this a necessity for a piece of software which is central to our
freedom and our participation in the technology commons. In the unfortunate
event that we cannot get such a commitment from the Emacs maintainers, we
will use an LLM-free Emacs regardless; it just won't be GNU Emacs.

Forks of Emacs have a poor track record, but this is a case where we are left
without much choice. There is too much at stake: giving up using Emacs for
many of us would feel tantamount to giving up on computing altogether, or at
least giving up on the parts of computing that bring us joy. Therefore we are
committed to build and maintain the Emacs we need to exist. We sincerely hope
that this will mean a renewed and re-invigorated commitment to GNU Emacs.

Our second goal is to reach others who feel this same concern, so you know
you are not alone. We have seen too many other communities fail to meet the
challenge of this moment, and we can't stand aside and observe the outcome of
this policy decision with cool detachment. If you feel the same way, join us
in solidarity. Visit https://human-emacs.org where you can read more
information and see the list of supporters so far; we would love to add your
name to the list or hear your voice in our IRC channel and mailing list.

My sincere thanks goes out to everyone who has brought Emacs to where it is
today and especially to those who have helped institute the policy that has
protected Emacs from LLM contributions so far. Emacs means too much to me to
see it share the same fate as Vim, Linux, rsync, and countless other
disappointments; we have to do better.

-Phil Hagelberg (aka technomancy), on behalf of the Human Emacs signatories

> concerns about the future of the project. As we see other projects
> adopting policies which accept LLM contributions, it's encouraged us to
> see that the current Emacs policy disallows them.
> 
> Our concerns stem from the temporary nature of the current policy, and its
> apparent focus on resolving legal uncertainty surrounding the copyright
> status of LLM-generated source code. Ensuring that the copyright of Emacs is
> legally defensible is of course important, but the way it appears to form the
> crux of the decision overlooks the harm posed to users and communities by
> acceptance of LLM-generated code. It would be a mistake to say that because
> something is legally allowable it must be acceptable, and for many of us who
> are deeply invested in Emacs, it's not a mistake we'd expect from this
> project's leadership.
> 
> Free Software is more than just code. It's built on a foundation of trust and
> solidarity. The greatest strength of Emacs is not its technology but the user
> base which shares and works together. As we watch this discussion play out in

> corrosive effect that LLMs have on trust and solidarity as soon as they are
> allowed in. We still have the chance to avoid that in the Emacs community and
> learn from the mistakes of others!
> 
> (At this point I should clarify that an argument about whether these harms
> are serious is not my interest; so if you are opening your MUA to compose a
> reply on these lines, please consider that we've heard all the arguments
> already and remain unmoved by them. We're not interested in arguments about
> how powerful they are, nor about how local models will some day address the
> problems, nor about how people can lie about the provenance of their
> patches; none of these are relevant. You're welcome to discuss this on a
> separate thread.)

Excuse me, but which harms are you talking about?  You mention them,
and claim they will mortally wound our solidarity and trust.  You also
threaten to fork Emacs unless the project accepts your views.  But you
haven't said a word about the harms themselves, let alone explained
how and why they are relevant to writing code for Emacs.

Given this lack of argumentation, the attempt to force the project to
accept your position (or else) is not very fair, to say the least.

> > Such policy discussions and decisions are not for the Emacs project to
> > make, they are global for all the GNU packages, and so here is the wrong
> > place to look for them.
> 
> While it's true that this mailing list cannot make policy decisions for all
> of GNU, there is no reason that Emacs could not hold itself to a higher
> standard than the rest of GNU.

Someone should first convincingly explain why what you demand is a
higher standard.  Without that, these arguments are not effective and
miss their point.

> First, we want the maintainers of Emacs to be aware of our intention to use
> a version of Emacs which is fully free from LLM contributions regardless of
> whether the current ban is made permanent or lifted. We invite those making
> the decision to look beyond copyright and weigh ALL the harms that lifting
> such a ban would inflict. Our fond hope is that upon consideration of the
> issues, Emacs maintainers will replace the temporary policy with a
> commitment to comprehensive prohibition of all LLM contributions including
> but not limited to code, documentation, commit messages.
> 
> We consider this a necessity for a piece of software which is central to our
> freedom and our participation in the technology commons. In the unfortunate
> event that we cannot get such a commitment from the Emacs maintainers, we
> will use an LLM-free Emacs regardless; it just won't be GNU Emacs.
> 
> Forks of Emacs have a poor track record, but this is a case where we are left
> without much choice. There is too much at stake: giving up using Emacs for
> many of us would feel tantamount to giving up on computing altogether, or at
> least giving up on the parts of computing that bring us joy. Therefore we are
> committed to build and maintain the Emacs we need to exist. We sincerely hope
> that this will mean a renewed and re-invigorated commitment to GNU Emacs.

You claim that using LLM-generated code in Emacs will harm the
community, and then you yourself announce your intention to harm the
community by forking the project.  That's a contradiction if I ever
saw one.

Bottom line: please be fair and honest enough to clarify the dangers
you see, and how they are so clear and present as to prompt you to
threaten a fork before any of these harmful developments even begin to
happen.  Without that, I don't think you have a right to request any
changes in our policies and procedures, let alone changes that will
require us to flatly reject valid and useful code based on its
provenance, something that the Emacs project (as well as the other GNU
projects) never did.

  > The TeX names would misdescribe the package, and I am not using them.
  > Knuth-Plass is the algorithm TeX uses for breaking lines, but the algorithm
  > is not TeX:

These names do not equate this algorithm with TeX.  What they do
is refer to the algorithm's way of breaking lines by pointing at TeX.

"TeX-line-break" is short for "line breaking, as in TeX".
"TeXlike-line-break" is short for "line breaking like the way TeX does
it."  Either of those names describes this package's purpose clearly
to anyone who has a basic awareness of what TeX does and what makes it
so good.  That is a large fraction of our community.

"TeX-fill" or "TeXlike-fill" would also achieve this.

By contrast, "ekp-fill" communicates only that it is about filling text.
Hardly anyone, even among TeX users, will know what "ekp" refers to.

I maintain that the names I propose, because they include "TeX", are
better for communicating what this package does.  That will help show
users what the package can do, and thus encourage its use.

It's not too late to adopt a name that refers to TeX.  Let's do that!
