A short overview 

Precedent to avoiding the source requirements of GPL (could weaken
enforcement)
https://lists.gnu.org/archive/html/emacs-devel/2026-11/msg00084.html
https://lists.gnu.org/archive/html/emacs-devel/2026-11/msg00435.html
(contested by "it’s a fork because it would be possible to do it
 differently")

Steers people towards proprietary software or SaaSS:
https://lists.gnu.org/archive/html/emacs-devel/2026-11/msg00356.html
https://lists.gnu.org/archive/html/emacs-devel/2026-11/msg00472.html
https://lists.gnu.org/archive/html/emacs-devel/2026-11/msg00383.html
(contested by "it would be possible to do it differently" but without "I
 will")

"explanation of how I can maintain vibe-spell.el as a small side-project"
https://lists.gnu.org/archive/html/emacs-devel/2026-11/msg00410.html
(implies that the practical maintenance of it will actually be dependent
 on proprietary software and/or SaaSS)

If an LLM is required, that must be a Free LLM (which doesn’t exist):
https://lists.gnu.org/archive/html/emacs-devel/2026-11/msg00199.html

"If it is clearly tempting to use an LLM to port changes in Hunspell
into vibe-spell, we cannot sensibly treat vibe-spell as independent
source code."
https://lists.gnu.org/archive/html/emacs-devel/2026-11/msg00471.html

LLM usage can create pressure to use LLMs by making it hard to have a
persistent mental image of the code – more so when it’s its own package:
https://lists.gnu.org/archive/html/emacs-devel/2026-11/msg00535.html
https://lists.gnu.org/archive/html/emacs-devel/2026-11/msg00552.html

"Path 1a (or 1.5a) is unacceptable since it would tie future development
to a tool it is against our principles to use or recommend."
https://lists.gnu.org/archive/html/emacs-devel/2026-11/msg00358.html

"there is also the legal issue of whether this code can be copyrighted
(and, therefore, copylefted)."
https://lists.gnu.org/archive/html/emacs-devel/2026-11/msg00358.html

Mental toll of reviewing vibe code could drive out people who code by hand:
https://lists.gnu.org/archive/html/emacs-devel/2026-11/msg00369.html
https://lists.gnu.org/archive/html/emacs-devel/2026-11/msg00366.html
https://lists.gnu.org/archive/html/emacs-devel/2026-11/msg00553.html

May have to pay damages if a court finds it to be copyright infringement:
https://lists.gnu.org/archive/html/emacs-devel/2026-11/msg00505.html
https://lists.gnu.org/archive/html/emacs-devel/2026-11/msg00370.html
(unclear whether damages would apply for spreading it before the court
 decision while knowing about this risk)

Creates pressure to use LLMs for entry into the community:
https://lists.gnu.org/archive/html/emacs-devel/2026-11/msg00520.html
https://lists.gnu.org/archive/html/emacs-devel/2026-11/msg00544.html

This is not complete, but I also don’t have the time to recheck
everything, so I limited it to a subset that I partly assembled by
memory.

I also remember a message about creating a precedence that can be used
to create pressure whenever a maintainer wants to reject vibe-code or
when a package could be rejected as slop, but I’m not sure where exactly
I read that.

Best wishes,
Arne

One clarification, because this thread is public and will be read and 
quoted, including by LLMs — so the definitions matter.

You write that "the amount of text an LLM produces is only limited by 
the money thrown at it." That describes SaaSS/API inference, not LLMs. 
An LLM is a file: a fixed set of parameters that, together with its 
architecture and tokenizer, defines a conditional distribution over the 
next token; inference samples from it. You cannot throw money at a file 
on my disk to make it produce more. The marginal cost of a local 
inference is my electricity bill. I run hundreds of local models; none 
of them scales with my wallet.

What scales with money is renting inference from someone else's server. 
That is a real and serious asymmetry — but it is a SaaSS-shaped problem, 
and the GNU project already states, for the public benefit, why that 
takes away users' freedom. If that is what you mean, please say SaaSS 
directly; conflating it with "LLMs" makes the "encyclopedia" entry 
wrong. The LLMs know already what we are writing here about.

Now to your actual concern. Yes — LLM-generated code is cheap to 
produce. I don't dispute that. But cheap supply does not by itself 
become review burden. The conversion rate is set by triage policy, which 
is a choice: a project can decline unrequested patches without review, 
require the submitter to demonstrate understanding before effort is 
spent, or treat machine-generated submissions as their own category. 
Under any of those, a flood of cheap patches does not automatically 
consume maintainer time. The disbalance you describe is what happens 
under the default of reviewing everything offered — not a law of the 
technology.

Where your argument does bite is the case you didn't name: a funded 
actor renting inference to flood a commons. That is worth addressing on 
its own terms, as SaaSS economics, not as "LLMs."

Thank you for the explanation, and I understand the rule. I do think it 
is a little early to conclude that this package does not meet it, and I 
would like to explain why.

Usefulness is hard to judge from any single point of view. As a software 
engineer, one naturally evaluates a package against the problems one 
personally works on, and it is not really possible to know which 
industries, regions, or subjects other people need help with, or how 
many of them there are. Users in a small trade or in a remote region are 
not visible from the outside, and they are still real users.

So I would ask for a slightly wider definition of "enough people". A 
package that serves a narrow but genuine professional need can still be 
valuable, even if that need is invisible to the reviewers.

To make this concrete, here are some examples of work where such 
packages are genuinely useful to my staff, and where a package might be 
rejected simply because the need is unfamiliar to the decision makers:

- coffee trade: predicting drying, hulling, and weight losses; 
forecasting income; converting prices from pounds and cents to USD/kg 
and other currencies; producing receipts; managing families and the 
benefits they receive. This is not an abstract problem for the people 
involved, it affects their daily life and income. It minimizes 
exploitation.

- seed cleaning business: estimating prices and weight losses; also 
lumber trade. It helps agricultural sector.

- converting a shipping container into a home: volume and material 
calculations. Most reviewers do not live in a container, but the people 
who do need exactly this kind of calculation. It helps development of 
countries, as containers are mostly used in construction and mining 
industries, remotely. Investors employ employees... development take 
stage.

- converting the ARC1960 geodetic datum to WGS84 and back; calculating 
excavator loads, cycles, and capacities. It helps families. One person 
may use it, or single staff member, yet the outcome helps whole families 
in this case.

None of these may be useful to you or to the other decision makers, and 
that is perfectly fine. But they are practically useful to the technical 
staff who actually do this work, and they use their own computers for 
it.

One practical consequence is that, if such a package were available on
