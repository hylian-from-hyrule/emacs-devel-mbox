I can provide one answer about risks: the Munich Court decided[1][2]
that for song lyrics, even if the use of some material as training data
is legal, it is still a copyrigt infringement if material generated by
an LLM reproduces a (potentially modified) version of parts of the
training data.

With the decision that the party responsible for that generation had to
pay a penalty and that it is not legal to exploit the generated
material (e.g. for money).

The responsible party as by the Munich decision was either the one
providing the model, or the one using it, or both to some degree,
depending on the complexity of the prompts. The more complex the
prompts, the more responsibility shifts to the user.

It is not clear, how courts will decide in the case of software. If they
follow the Munich decision, then any substantial part of code that’s too
similar to code in the training data violates the copyright of that part
of the training data (if no permission to reuse that material is given).

In a recent telecon by FSFE and nlnet[3], an FSFE lawyer said that the
legal situation is currently very much in flux, so no one knows what
the legal situation will be in a few years.

Yes, a free LLM that runs locally could absolutely be adapted so it does 
not do this, and that is exactly why the problem should be solved at the 
model level instead of just banning contributions. The failures 
mentioned happened with proprietary, hosted models, where the community 
could not inspect, change, or constrain the tool at all. A genuinely

free-software movement should actually do: push to build the free LLM, 
not treat LLMs as inherently corrosive. That is the same move GNU made 
with proprietary tools — build the free replacement instead of banning 
the use.

I think you've misunderstood what Richard wanted to convey.  Note that

interpretation of this is that we should not be _forced_ to use the
_same_ LLM to continue developing such code.

However, it is quite possible that someone other than the person who
submitted the code will, at some future date, submit further changes
and improvements for that code, and that other person could use LLM to
produce or assist in production of these further changes.  If
LLM-generated code is acceptable at all, there's no practical way to
ensure this will not happen, because, once admitted into Emacs, the
original LLM-generated code is not different from any other code in
Emacs.  And it is unreasonable to expect the Emacs project to track,
for each line of Emacs sources, whether they were at some point in the
past modified by LLM-generated changes, and then disallow any further
LLM-generated changes for those lines.

IOW, what I think Richard says is that we should not accept the
situation where some code in Emacs _must_ be developed or maintained
using LLMs, but there's no objection to _allow_ such LLM-generated
changes if that's one of the alternatives, alongside changes produced
entirely by humans.

> I believe this constitutes "pressure", and is a danger to the 
> wider (potential) community, even though it is probably not a 
> feeling experienced by maintainers in the same position.

I don't see how a possibility (but not necessity) of using LLMs could
be considered "pressure".  Such a conclusion is against my intuitive
understanding of what constitutes "pressure".  For any aspect like
this to constitute "pressure", the alternatives must be either
forbidden or at least punitive or much less attractive to _everyone_.
Like, for example, our clear preference to extend Emacs in Lisp rather
than in C.  That some people might decide, given their personal
situations and preferences, to use this or that alternative, does not
come close to "pressure" in my book.

My own experience of this kind of "pressure" is in a slightly 
different area: use of Emacs's Info pages. I know from experience 
that not only are many (newish) Emacs users unaware of the Info 
pages; they resist using the Info pages once becoming aware. There 
are various factors to this, but perhaps the biggest is the 
availability of the documents on the web.

The web is a familiar form of access (viewing, and somewhat more 
significantly, searching) and although it's an unusual way to view 
the behaviour I would suggest the availability of the web 
constitutes "pressure" to use it INSTEAD of the Info pages.

do not feel this "pressure" at all. But I see it clearly in the 
behaviour of other users (and regularly try to change their minds 
about it).

elisp will do the same; users will feel "pressure" not to write 
elisp themselves. This is so clear to me that I don't even think 
it's a point that needs to be established; I regard it as fact.

on LLMs in the Emacs community, and a decrease in actual 
understanding of elisp, constitutes harm to the community.

> much less attractive to _everyone_.

On 2026-11-18 at 21:30, <rms@gnu.org> wrote:

> I wonder if a free LLM, usable locally, could make it possible to
> adapt the LLM such that it won't do this.

There is no such thing as a “free LLM”. LLMs are made out of:

(0) Program. The model as an executable might be gratis, i.e.
downloadable without charge. Nice.

(1) Code. The source code might be free. Nice!

(2) Expertise. Big Tech increasingly isolates their respective AI
research and does not share it publicly. Still, for the clever
hacker community, this might be the least of problems.

(3) Data. The amount of data needed to train an LLM to a usable
level of intelligence is not available to the public. There are
public datasets to download but they are too small. You could
scrape the web but nowadays many websites require proof of work,
cookies and Javascript, i.e. a full browser, which makes it
unscrapable without a data centers or botnets. Big Tech companies
like Meta and Google (which cooperates with Reddit) do their best
to prevent scraping of their walled gardens so that they can keep
their data to train their own *large* language models.

A model might be pre-trained and ‘open weight’, but if the
training and post-training data is missing, you cannot bootstrap
the weights yourself. You'll still dependent on the actor that has
trained the model with the data.

(4) Compute. To train a model on the data, you need enormous
amount of compute that is only possible with giant data centers.
You cannot do this as a loosely organized collective of hackers.
But you'll depend on data centers of Big Tech.

Note that the compute power you need for inference, i.e. to ‘run
the model locally’ is something different: Many models run on
computers that cost around 5000 USD (if you buy graphics cards
second hand) and are thus affordable for some well-earning, senior
software engineers in the Global North.


“Free LLM” is a hypothetical, utopian term. Questions like ‘if we
had a free, local LLM, would ...?’ thus don't make sense to me.
